# Editorial patterns

Read this reference for audits and for standard or deep rewrites of any prose. Treat the
patterns as diagnoses, not automatic violations. Context, genre, authorial voice, and
frequency determine whether an instance needs editing.

## How to use the patterns

Find the small number of repeated habits that make the draft feel generic or performed.
Fix the underlying habit across the text instead of replacing isolated words. Preserve an
instance when it is accurate, natural in the author's voice, or structurally useful.

In audit mode, report only material patterns. Use this shape:

```text
Pattern — "short excerpt"
Why: concrete effect on this draft.
Direction: concise way to fix it.
```

Group repetitions. Do not count occurrences, assign an AI probability, or infer who wrote
the text.

## Framing and structure

### Throat-clearing

The opening announces honesty, clarity, context, or an intention to explain before making
the point. Remove the setup when the next sentence works by itself. Keep it when it reveals
real hesitation, relationship, or voice.

### Manufactured insight

The draft claims that everyone overlooks a point or that the author alone understands it.
Examples include setups about what nobody tells you or what most people get wrong. Make
the claim directly and let its evidence create the insight.

### Binary revelation

The text repeatedly uses `not X, but Y`, `this is not X; it is Y`, or a sequence of rejected
labels before revealing the preferred one. State the actual relationship directly unless the
contrast corrects a likely misunderstanding.

### Rhetorical self-answer

The author asks a question only to answer it in the next few words. Convert it to a direct
statement unless the question creates genuine suspense or follows the author's spoken
cadence.

### Colon reveal

A dramatic setup uses a colon to unveil a short claim: `The real problem: nobody owns it.`
Write a normal sentence when the colon contributes only theater. Keep colons for lists,
labels, quotations, and genuinely clarifying expansions.

### Premature map

The introduction describes what the text will cover even though the piece is short enough
to begin. Remove the itinerary unless readers need it to navigate a long or technical
document.

### Recap ending

The final paragraph repeats the introduction or summarizes points the reader just read.
End on the last concrete consequence, decision, unresolved pressure, or next action.

### Manufactured profundity

The last line converts a specific point into a broad metaphor, aphorism, or mic-drop. Delete
it when it adds mood but no meaning. Do not replace it with a more polished slogan.

## Claims and reasoning

### Interpretive metacommentary

The text tells readers that a point is important, surprising, subtle, or worth noticing
instead of showing the consequence. Delete the label when the evidence already carries the
weight. Otherwise supply the missing mechanism or consequence.

### Decorative analysis

A trailing clause claims that a fact is `highlighting`, `demonstrating`, `underscoring`,
`reflecting`, or `showcasing` a broad virtue. Replace it with a causal consequence or remove
it. The grammar is not the problem; unsupported interpretation is.

### Importance inflation

The draft calls an ordinary event pivotal, transformative, vital, groundbreaking, or a
testament to something without establishing the stakes. State what changed, for whom, by
how much, or for the first time.

### Floating attribution

Claims hide behind unnamed experts, reports, observers, studies, or conventional wisdom.
Name the source already available in the material, qualify the claim honestly, or remove
it. Never invent a source.

### Abstract benefit

The text promises efficiency, innovation, flexibility, impact, or better experience without
showing the mechanism. Replace the abstraction with an existing fact: time saved, steps
removed, failure avoided, capability added, or behavior changed.

### Forced completeness

Every claim receives caveats, both sides, and a neat resolution even when the author has a
clear view. Keep material uncertainty and tradeoffs, but do not fabricate balance to sound
reasonable.

## Sentence-level habits

### Inflated verb phrase

Several words perform the job of one: `make a decision`, `has the ability to`, `provides an
opportunity to`. Prefer `decide`, `can`, or the concrete action when the meaning survives.

### Fake-strong verb

The sentence avoids plain verbs by saying a product `serves as`, an event `marks`, or a
feature `enables` when `is`, `has`, or a specific action is clearer. Choose precision over
energy.

### Synonym cycling

The same entity becomes the platform, solution, tool, system, and offering across adjacent
sentences. Repeat the correct term. Lexical variety is not worth referential ambiguity.

### Stacked fragments

Several short fragments manufacture urgency: `No delay. No debate. Just results.` Combine
them when the rhythm is performing confidence rather than expressing the author's natural
cadence.

### Over-segmented thought

A paragraph breaks one continuous thought into a row of short declarative sentences or
independent clauses, sometimes attaching the final contrast with a semicolon. Each sentence
may be grammatical and different in length; the problem is that every clause receives the
same tidy pause. Join clauses that belong together with a conjunction or subordination that
makes the relationship explicit. Keep a short sentence when it earns emphasis, not merely
because it makes the paragraph look clear.

### Uniform cadence

Sentences share the same length, opening, clause count, or punchline shape. Change only
enough of them to follow the actual hierarchy of ideas. Variation should carry emphasis,
not imitate randomness.

### Qualification fog

Words such as `just`, `actually`, `really`, `simply`, `clearly`, `fundamentally`, and
`importantly` accumulate without changing the claim. Remove empty instances while keeping
ones that express real contrast, degree, uncertainty, or voice.

### Punctuation as rhythm generator

Em dashes, colons, parentheses, and semicolons repeat because the model is using
punctuation to create cadence. Keep every mark that clarifies the sentence. Remove clusters
and vary the sentence structure instead of enforcing a numeric limit.

A lone semicolon can still feel conspicuous in otherwise plain or conversational prose when
`and`, `but`, `while`, or `though` would express the relationship more naturally. Treat that
as a voice mismatch, not proof that semicolons are inherently artificial.

## Formatting habits

### Decorative hierarchy

Emoji headings, bold fragments, tiny titled sections, and unnecessary callouts decorate a
weak structure. Use hierarchy only when it helps readers navigate or compare information.

### List compulsion

The draft turns every set of two or three related thoughts into bullets. Use prose when the
ideas form one argument; use a list when the items are genuinely parallel or scannability
matters.

### Symmetric sections

Every section has the same length and internal shape regardless of importance. Let section
size follow the material.

## Phrase watch list

Watch for language such as `delve`, `tapestry`, `realm`, `beacon`, `unlock`, `elevate`,
`empower`, `seamless`, `robust`, `transformative`, `game-changing`, `ever-evolving`,
`it is worth noting`, `at its core`, `in today's world`, `the reality is`, and `let's dive in`.

None of these is banned. Keep a word when it is precise, established terminology, quoted,
or natural for the author. Change it when it delays the point, inflates an ordinary claim,
or substitutes for a concrete fact.

## Final checks

### Portability test

Could a sentence move unchanged to another person, company, product, or situation? If so,
it probably needs a subject-specific fact, mechanism, consequence, or judgment. Some
functional connective text may remain portable; do not force specificity into every line.

### Evidence test

Does each strong judgment have support in the supplied material, or is tone doing the work
of evidence?

### Deletion test

If an interpretive sentence disappears, does the reader lose meaning or only an instruction
about how to react? Delete the latter.

### Rhythm test

Read the text aloud. Do pauses, fragments, long sentences, and paragraph breaks follow the
thought, or do they recur as a template?

### Ending test

Remove the final sentence temporarily. If the piece becomes sharper and loses no required
action or information, leave it out.

### Recognition test

Would the author recognize their own vocabulary, edge, uncertainty, humor, digressions,
and level of polish? If the edit sounds like a different competent writer, restore the
author's choices.
